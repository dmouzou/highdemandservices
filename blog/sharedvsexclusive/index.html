Shared leads vs exclusive leads: what actually changes

Shared leads go to several contractors at once. Exclusive leads go to one. Here is what that changes for your calls, your close rate and your cost per job.

Home / Blog

4 min read

The short version

A shared lead is one homeowner request sold to several businesses. An exclusive lead is sold to one. The price per lead is almost always lower for shared leads, and that is the whole appeal. The catch is that price per lead is not the number that pays your bills. Cost per job won is.

This guide explains how each model works, what it feels like on the phone, where each one makes sense and how to compare them using your own numbers instead of a sales pitch.

How shared leads work

When a homeowner submits a request, the provider sends it to a handful of contractors, often three to five, within seconds. Each contractor pays for the same contact. The homeowner's phone starts ringing, and some people stop answering after the second or third call. Others pick up, hear the same opening line from four different companies and start treating the whole thing as a price comparison.

The contractor who calls first has an edge, and the one who quotes lowest has another. Both edges push you toward racing: dropping everything to call back within a minute and trimming your quote before you have even seen the job. You are competing on speed and price, two things that are hard to sustain.

How exclusive leads work

An exclusive lead goes to one business only. For phone calls, that means the call rings your phone and nobody else's. The homeowner expects to hear from you and is not fielding a stack of competing calls. They may still talk to other businesses, but they are not being chased by them at the same moment.

The conversation tends to be different. Instead of defending your price against two unseen competitors, you can ask questions, explain your process and book a visit. You are one of the businesses they will compare later, not one of five fighting over the same first minute. That changes the tone of the call, and it often changes the outcome.

The real comparison: cost per job won

Lead price tells you what you pay to receive a contact. It says nothing about what you pay to win work. To compare fairly, work out cost per job won for each source. The formula is simple: cost per lead divided by close rate.

Here is an illustration. Suppose a shared lead costs $30 and you close 8% of them. That is $30 divided by 0.08, or $375 of lead spend per job won. Now suppose an exclusive call costs $60 and you close 25%. That is $60 divided by 0.25, or $240 per job. The exclusive source costs twice as much per lead and about a third less per job.

Those figures are examples, not promises. Your close rates will differ, and that is exactly the point: measure them for each source before you decide.

The hidden costs of shared leads

Shared leads carry costs that rarely show up on an invoice:

  • Time spent chasing callbacks and leaving voicemails that are never returned
  • Discounting to win a price race, which cuts margin on every job you do win
  • The mental cost for the person making ten calls to book one visit
  • Slower response to your best leads because the phone is busy with the worst ones

There is also a reputation cost. If a homeowner is annoyed by a flood of calls, some of that annoyance lands on whoever they speak to first or last. Exclusive delivery avoids the problem by design.

Where exclusive leads can disappoint

Exclusive does not mean guaranteed. A homeowner who calls you may still get quotes from others, and some will never book anyone. Exclusive also usually costs more per lead, so a weak phone routine hurts more. If your callbacks are slow or your quotes are vague, paying for exclusivity will not fix that.

It also does not mean every lead is good. Providers define exclusive differently. Some mean the lead goes to one contractor in your trade. Others mean it goes to one contractor per zip code per day. Ask for the definition in writing.

When shared leads still make sense

Shared leads can work for businesses with spare capacity, a fast-response routine and a cost structure that supports low prices. Some contractors use them for filler work during slow weeks and keep exclusive sources for the jobs they most want. A new business with little to lose may also use shared leads to learn what customers ask for.

If you use both, track them separately from day one. Give each source its own tracking number or tag so you can compare booked visits, jobs won and revenue side by side after a month.

Questions to ask any provider

Before you sign with any lead source, get clear answers to these:

  • Who else receives this lead or call, exactly?
  • How do you check that it is a real person in my service area?
  • What happens when a lead is invalid, and how fast do I get a credit?
  • Is there a contract, a minimum or a setup fee?
  • Can I see every lead with its time, source and outcome?
  • Is pricing published, or do I have to ask for a quote?

Clear, written answers up front are a good sign. Vague answers are information too.

Key takeaways

  • Shared leads often become price races
  • Exclusive leads let you focus on one conversation
  • Compare sources on cost per job won, not price per lead
